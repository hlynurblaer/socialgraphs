Overview

193 UN member states, one node each, with a directed edge A → B when A's Wikipedia article links to B's. Built and computed exactly as described in build_un_network.ipynb and analyze_network.py — re-run the latter any time the edge list changes to refresh these numbers.

Every one of the 193 member states reaches every other, ignoring direction — no isolates, no small clique sitting off on its own, unlike the Marvel network from week 1 (17 disconnected characters and a self-contained 9-node clique). Follow the arrows, though, and the picture changes: only 192 of 193 countries form a single strongly connected component, where every country can reach every other by following outgoing links and be reached in turn. Timor-Leste is the one exception, and the reason is stark: its article links out to 7 other countries (Australia, Germany, Indonesia, Philippines, Portugal, Sweden, Yemen), all of them inside the giant component, so Timor-Leste can reach the core just fine — but its in-degree is zero. Not one of the other 192 countries' articles links back to it, so nothing can reach Timor-Leste in return. A single missing direction is all it takes to fall outside a strongly connected component, which is exactly why the course draws a hard line between weak and strong connectivity for directed graphs.

Reciprocity is the more surprising number here. At 5.9% density, if links were placed independently at random, you'd expect roughly a 5.9% chance that any given link's reverse also exists — density and baseline reciprocity should track each other under pure chance. The actual reciprocity is 43.6%, about seven times that baseline: when one country's article links to another, there's nearly even odds the link runs back the other way too. Wikipedia's link structure isn't random here — it's tracking real, mostly-mutual relationships (shared borders, colonial history, regional blocs), not one-off mentions.

Paths & Distances

Computed on the undirected giant component (G.to_undirected(), largest connected component) — same treatment exercise 3.3 gives the Marvel network, since path length and diameter need a graph where every reachable pair actually has a path to measure.

An average path length of 2.19 makes this a small world by any definition: pick two countries at random and there's typically only one other country's worth of separation between their Wikipedia articles. The diameter of 4 means even the two most distant countries in the network are never more than four hops apart, and a radius of 2 — with the United Kingdom and United States sitting at the center — means both of them are within two hops of literally every other member state. That's an unusually tight bound for 193 nodes, and it lines up with the centrality leaderboards below: the same two countries top or near-top nearly every measure of importance in the network.

Centrality Leaderboards

Top 15 by each measure, with the underlying value shown — not just a bare ranking. Each measure answers a different question about a country's position in the network.

The same handful of great powers — the United States, United Kingdom, China, Russia, Germany and France — dominate every centrality measure except one. They're the most linked-to (in-degree), the best-connected to other well-connected countries (eigenvector), the ones other shortest paths most often pass through (betweenness — where the UK actually edges out the US, 0.125 to 0.102), and the ones Wikipedia's own link structure treats as most authoritative (PageRank). Out-degree flips the picture entirely: the busiest linkers aren't world powers at all, they're smaller countries like Lithuania, São Tomé and Príncipe and Kazakhstan, whose own articles simply name a lot of other countries (regional neighbors, colonial history, diplomatic relations) without being nearly as often cited back. It's the same fame-versus-chattiness split week 1 found in the Marvel network, just with countries standing in for superheroes.

Cliques

A clique is a set of countries that are all pairwise linked to each other. Two different, deliberately separate notions of "linked" are used below, since they answer different questions and give very different answers.

Both maximum all-links cliques are regionally coherent rather than a random assortment of well-connected countries: one is a Pacific/Anglosphere cluster built around Fiji, Kiribati, Solomon Islands, Tonga and Tuvalu alongside their historical ties to the United Kingdom, Australia and New Zealand; the other is a Middle Eastern cluster of Iraq, Israel, Lebanon, Saudi Arabia, Sudan and the United Arab Emirates, with either Egypt or Iran filling the seventh seat. Tighten the requirement to mutual-only links and the clique number drops from 7 to 5 — and only the five Pacific microstates (Fiji, Solomon Islands, Tonga, Tuvalu, Vanuatu) survive intact. The UK, Australia and New Zealand all drop out: their articles get cited by those island nations, but the reverse links aren't dense enough to keep them in a fully reciprocal clique. What's left is a small, tight regional bloc that genuinely writes about each other, not just a set of countries that happen to share a common reference point.
